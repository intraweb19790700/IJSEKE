Abstract: Hospital  complaints,  open-ended  survey  responses,  and  online  reviews  describe  problems  in communication, waiting, billing, and administrative services. Their analysis can support service improvement, but  the  text  may  contain  personal  identifiers.  Existing  lightweight  classifiers  generally  treat  redaction  as  a separate  preprocessing  step  and  do  not  explicitly  constrain  predictions  to  remain  stable  after  identifiers  are removed. We propose RC-EdgeDistill, a redaction-consistent learning method that uses supervision from a large language model (LLM) to train a compact model for hospital feedback analysis at the edge. The LLM generates weak labels for service-issue category, sentiment, review priority, and personal-information risk. A multi-task student  learns  from  paired  original  and  redacted  texts,  while  a  symmetric  Kullback-Leibler  (KL)  objective constrains the two views to produce similar predictions for the three semantic tasks. New feedback is redacted locally before CPU inference. Experiments on 2,000 hospital reviews and related public-web records showed that  RC-EdgeDistill  matched  the  strongest  evaluated  CPU  classifier  in  mean  macro-F1  while  increasing redaction consistency from 95.33% to 98.67%. Its mean macro-F1 was also 16.64% higher than that of the best frozen  sentence-embedding  baseline.  The  model  and  vectorizer  occupied  22.8  MB,  and  the  complete  local pipeline  processed  191  records  per  second.  The  findings  indicate  that  LLM-derived  supervision  can  train  a compact model for standard CPUs and that redaction-consistency learning reduces prediction changes caused by masking  personal  identifiers  while  preserving  classification  performance.  By  restricting  the  LLM  to  label construction  and  assigning  routine  analysis  to  the  local  student,  RC-EdgeDistill  provides  a  reproducible  and auditable edge-processing approach for hospital feedback.

Keywords: edge intelligence; large language models; weak supervision; hospital feedback analysis; redaction consistency; multi-task learning; personal-information detection

## 1. Introduction

Complaints,  open-ended  survey  responses,  online  reviews,  and  public  posts  provide  detailed  evidence  of communication failures, long waits, billing disputes, and administrative delays that structured ratings may not capture [1,2]. Such feedback is collected through both public platforms and hospital complaint, survey, hotline, and follow-up systems. Institution-owned records are more likely to contain names, contact details, visit times, or staff identifiers and may therefore need to be processed within local data-governance boundaries. This study aims  to  identify  the  principal  service  issue  in  each  record  and  determine  its  review  priority,  producing  four outputs: service-issue category, sentiment, review priority, and personal-information risk. Previous studies have used NLP for patient-experience analysis and complaint classification [3,4], as well as sentiment and aspectbased  analysis  [5,6].  However,  these  approaches  commonly  separate  label  construction,  personal-information handling, and text classification, and few explicitly constrain a lightweight model to preserve its decision after identifiers are masked. The central problem is therefore to perform multi-output feedback analysis locally while maintaining prediction stability after redaction.

To  address  this  problem,  we  propose  RC-EdgeDistill,  an  LLM-supervised  redaction-consistent  learning method for hospital feedback at the edge. LLMs have demonstrated the ability to generate structured semantic judgments  in  healthcare  text  processing  [8,9]  and  automated  annotation  [10,11].  RC-EdgeDistill  uses  this capability  only  during  model  development  to  construct  weak-supervision  labels  for  historical  records.  A compact multi-task student then learns from paired original and redacted texts. A symmetric Kullback-Leibler (KL) objective aligns the  prediction  distributions  of  the  two  views  for  service-issue  category,  sentiment,  and review priority,  while  a  deterministic  local  module  detects  and  masks  personal  information  before  inference. This division of roles transfers label-level supervision from the LLM to a CPU-deployable student [15,17] and allows routine analysis to remain close to the data source [39,40].

This work comprises three connected contributions. First, it formulates prediction stability before and after redaction as an explicit training objective, using paired text views and a consistency loss to constrain changes caused by identifier  masking.  Second,  it  implements  label-based  teacher-student  transfer:  the  LLM  generates structured  supervision  labels  without  requiring  access  to  hidden  states  or  soft  logits.  Third,  it  separates deterministic personal-information rules from the learned classifier so that the two components can be inspected, tested,  and  updated  independently.  Experiments  on  1,049  de-identified  Chinese  public-web  records  and  951 English public hospital reviews compared RC-EdgeDistill with a rule model, sparse CPU classifiers, and frozen MiniLM,  multilingual  E5,  and  BGE-small  representations.  RC-EdgeDistill  matched  the  strongest  evaluated CPU  classifier  in  mean  macro-F1  while  increasing  redaction  consistency  from  95.33%  to  98.67%.  It  also exceeded the best frozen embedding baseline by 16.64% in mean macro-F1. The model and vectorizer occupied 22.8  MB, and the complete local pipeline processed 191 records per second. These results support the  main conclusions concerning redaction stability and lightweight CPU execution.

Section 2 reviews hospital feedback analysis, LLM-assisted annotation, privacy-preserving NLP, and edge intelligence. Section 3 defines the task and presents RC-EdgeDistill. Section 4 describes the experiments and results. Section 5 discusses the methodological and practical implications, and Section 6 concludes the paper.

## 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Hospital Feedback Text Analysis

Patient comments, complaints, and open-ended responses complement structured quality indicators. Recent studies  have  used  these  texts  to  identify  concerns  involving  communication,  access,  staff  responsiveness, facilities, and administration [4,6]. One scoping review documented supervised and unsupervised NLP methods for patient-experience text [1]. Another examined sentiment analysis of comments collected from online sources and surveys [2]. The evidence supports the analytical value of written feedback, although operational use still requires labels that correspond to hospital response processes.

Most  patient-feedback  NLP  pipelines  focus  on  sentiment,  latent  topics,  or  broad  experience  categories. Sentiment does not identify the responsible service area, and unsupervised topics may not align with operational categories.  Supervised  models  can  learn  those  categories  directly,  but  annotation  becomes  costly  when  the schema  includes  service-issue  category,  sentiment,  review  priority,  and  personal-information  risk.  These constraints motivate a multi-output task rather than sentiment classification alone.

Hospital  feedback  may  contain  care-process  descriptions,  departments,  dates,  and  personal  identifiers.  A useful  model  must  preserve  service  meaning  while  limiting  exposure  of  identifying  details.  Recent  analyses show that written patient feedback can support service-issue classification as well as more detailed modeling of sentiment and emotion [4,7]. On this basis, the present task targets actionable service-issue and priority labels rather than an aggregate service-quality score.

Previous  studies  generally  examine  feedback  classification,  sentiment,  or  patient-experience  measurement separately.  Few  jointly  consider  weak-supervision  label  construction,  prediction  stability  after  redaction,  and low-cost local inference. RC-EdgeDistill integrates these requirements.

## 2.2 LLM-Assisted Annotation and Small-Model Learning

LLMs offer a practical means of generating structured labels for text classification. They can perform well on selected annotation tasks [10], and recent work identifies annotation and data construction as important uses of  LLMs  in  computational  research  [12].  Their  reliability  nevertheless  varies  with  the  task,  prompt,  class structure,  and  evaluation  criteria  [11].  The  labels  used  here  are  therefore  treated  as  LLM-assisted  weak supervision, not as human expert annotation.

Weak-supervision research emphasizes that automatically generated labels should be assessed in relation to their  source  and  intended  task  [13].  Healthcare  LLM  evaluation  frameworks likewise call for explicit criteria and  task-specific  assessment  [14].  Accordingly,  two  instructions  with  different  emphases  are  used,  and  their disagreement rate is recorded. This rate measures sensitivity to instruction design rather than agreement between human annotators.

Knowledge distillation provides a basis  for  transferring  capability  from  large  to  compact  models  [16,17]. Recent  surveys  classify  LLM  compression  and  distillation  methods  by  transferred  knowledge,  student architecture, and optimization objective [18,21]. Broader model-compression reviews cover pruning, quantization, low-rank approximation, and distillation [19], while evaluations of compact biomedical transformers demonstrate the practical value of smaller language models in health-related NLP [20]. Parameterefficient  online  distillation  has  also  been  studied  for  pretrained  language  models  [23].  Conventional  logit distillation typically requires soft probability outputs from the teacher, and some methods also use hidden states. RC-EdgeDistill accesses neither form of internal information; its supervision consists solely of structured labels generated by an LLM. It is therefore a label-based form of teacher-student transfer rather than standard logit distillation [22].

Sentence-BERT  and  E5  provide  general  or  weakly  supervised  sentence  representations  [27,28],  while multilingual E5 and BGE extend coverage to multilingual and Chinese settings [29,30]. Recent reviews have also  examined  how  embedding  choices  affect  downstream  text  analytics  [31].  Frozen  embeddings  with lightweight classifiers are relevant low-resource baselines, but their pretraining objectives are not aligned with the  hospital  feedback  label  schema  and  do  not  enforce  redaction  consistency.  RC-EdgeDistill  optimizes  both requirements directly.

Consistency  regularization  encourages  stable  predictions  under  perturbed,  augmented,  or  partially  labeled views. Recent semi-supervised methods continue to use prediction consistency and confidence-aware training [24,25], while virtual adversarial training provides a foundational formulation for local prediction smoothness [26]. RC-EdgeDistill applies this principle to text redaction. Because redaction removes identifiers but should preserve  service  meaning,  symmetric  KL  divergence  between  original  and  redacted  predictions  makes invariance an explicit training objective.

## 2.3 Privacy-Preserving and Edge Healthcare NLP

Healthcare text de-identification has used rule-based, machine-learning, and neural methods, as summarized in a recent systematic review [32]. Contemporary evaluations have examined both established de-identification tools and pretrained transformer systems across clinical text types [33,34]. GPT-based de-identification has also been  evaluated,  although  accuracy  and  disclosure  risk  remain  task  dependent  [35].  MIMIC-IV  illustrates  the continuing  role  of  access  control  and  de-identification  in  secondary  research  [36].  Hospital  feedback  differs from  electronic  health  records  but  can  contain  the  same  types  of  identifying  information,  including  names, contact details, locations, and dates.

Privacy-preserving  healthcare  AI  includes  de-identification,  federated  learning,  secure  computation,  and local  processing  [37].  Recent  guidance  for  trustworthy  and  deployable  healthcare  AI  also  treats  privacy, robustness,  governance,  and  lifecycle  monitoring  as  linked  requirements  [38].  These  approaches  address different  stages:  federated  learning  distributes  training,  de-identification  removes  identifying  text,  and  edge inference  determines  where  predictions  are  computed.  RC-EdgeDistill  focuses  on  local  personal-information handling and CPU inference for new feedback.

Edge  intelligence  places  inference  close  to  the  data  source  to  reduce  communication  overhead  and dependence  on  centralized  services  [39].  Recent  reviews  describe  compression,  scheduling,  and  system techniques  for  deploying  LLM  capabilities  in  resource-constrained  edge  environments  [40],  together  with federated and edge learning strategies for large language models [41]. Healthcare-focused reviews examine the same  principle  under  heterogeneous  resource,  connectivity,  and  privacy  constraints  [42].  Although  hospital feedback is not a sensor stream, internal complaints and survey responses originate in local information systems. A  CPU-deployable  model  can  classify  these  records  locally  while  preserving  institutional  control  over preprocessing rules and model versions.

Table  1  compares  the  main  technical  routes  for  this  task.  Direct  cloud  LLM  inference  provides  flexible semantic processing but requires transmission of the text being analyzed. Rule models are transparent and fast but  have  limited  linguistic  coverage.  TF-IDF  classifiers  are  suitable  for  CPU  inference  but  lack  a  redactionconsistency objective. Frozen embeddings provide general semantic representations without explicitly modeling redaction. RC-EdgeDistill combines LLM weak supervision, redaction-consistent training, and local inference.

## 4.1 Dataset and Experimental Setting

The dataset contains 2,000 records with LLM-assisted weak-supervision labels: 1,049 de-identified Chinese public-web  records  and  951  English  public  hospital  reviews.  The  Chinese  subset  comprises  hospital-related public-web  excerpts,  including  direct  comments  and  other  public  accounts.  The  English  subset  was  obtained after duplicate removal from version 1 of the Hospital Reviews Dataset by  junaid6731 [43]. According to its Kaggle documentation, the reviews were collected from Google Maps pages for hospitals in Bengaluru, India, and are distributed under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license. These public and de-identified sources enable reproducible evaluation.  Hospital-owned  complaints,  surveys,  hotline  records,  and  follow-up  notes  remain  the  intended application sources.

The training, validation, and test sets contain 1,400, 300, and 300 records. The validation set includes 158 Chinese  and  142  English  records,  and  the  test  set  includes  160  Chinese  and  140  English  records.  Synthetic templates  were  appended  to  training  records  with  a  configured  probability  of  0.35  to  increase  coverage  of common identifier patterns. The templates represented email addresses, telephone numbers, patient identifiers, identity  numbers,  and  addresses.  No  synthetic  identifiers  were  added  to  the  validation  or  test  sets.  Semantic labels  remained  unchanged.  The  original-view  risk  target  was  set  to  contains\_pii,  and  paired  views  were generated by the current redaction function.

The TF-IDF vocabularies  were  fitted  only  on  original  and  redacted  training  views.  The  final  model  uses 10,000 input features, comprising 6,000 character n-gram and 4,000 word n-gram TF-IDF features. The student has a 384-dimensional shared layer with 0.15 dropout. It was trained for 160 full-batch epochs using Adam, a learning rate of 0.006, weight decay of 10 -4 , and random seed 20260715.

Every record was processed by the two-instruction LLM labeling and checking workflow. The instructions disagreed on service-issue category for 184 of 2,000 records (9.20%). Disagreement rates for sentiment, priority, and personal-information risk were 0.00%, 0.15%, and 0.00%, respectively. Clinical  process,  other/uncertain,  and  staff  communication contained  582,  445,  and  318  records,  respectively,  whereas  medication  contained  only  17.  Counts  for  the remaining categories ranged from 74 to 185. The dataset included 874 positive, 769 negative, and 357 neutral records, together with 1,023 low-, 464 medium-, and 513 high-priority records. ## 4.3 Main Classification Performance

On the held-out test set, RC-EdgeDistill with 𝜆 = 0.05 and the local personal-information module achieved a composite macro-F1 of 0.6896. LinearSVM was the closest CPU baseline at 0.6877, whereas RC-EdgeDistill achieved higher redaction consistency.

In  contrast,  the  mean  consistency  difference  was  0.0333,  with  a  95%  bootstrap  interval  from 0.0155 to 0.0522. ## 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Methodological Implications

RC-EdgeDistill assigns distinct roles to the LLM and the local student. During model development, the LLM constructs  structured  weak-supervision  labels;  during  routine  use,  the  student  analyzes  new  feedback.  This separation uses the LLM's semantic labeling capability without requiring an external model call for every new record.  It  also  allows  the  label  schema  and  inference  model  to  be  versioned,  regression-tested,  and  reviewed within the institution independently.

Redaction consistency is the central methodological contribution. RC-EdgeDistill learns from original and redacted views and penalizes divergence between their prediction distributions. Redaction is part of the intended processing path, so service-issue decisions should remain stable after identifiers are removed. In the ablation, increasing 𝜆 from  0  to  0.05  raised  composite  macro-F1  from  0.6818  to  0.6896  and  mean  consistency  from 0.9844 to 0.9867. The bootstrap analysis did not distinguish its composite macro-F1 from LinearSVM, but it did support  higher  mean  consistency:  98.67%  versus  95.33%,  with  a  95%  difference  interval  of  1.55  to  5.22 percentage points.

The four outputs are related but not interchangeable. A neutral record may still expose personal information, while  negative  billing  and  communication  records  require  action  by  different  service  units.  A  shared representation  with  separate  task  heads  provides  service-issue  category,  sentiment,  priority,  and  personalinformation risk rather than a single polarity label.

None  of  the  three  frozen  embedding  baselines  exceeded  the  sparse  multi-task  model  on  this  dataset. Character  and  word  n-grams  directly  capture  service  phrases,  redaction  placeholders,  and  short-text  patterns. RC-EdgeDistill adds task-specific supervision and an explicit consistency objective. The comparison applies to the evaluated dataset, frozen representations, and downstream classifiers. Task-specific encoder fine-tuning may produce different results.

## 5.2 Practical Deployment Value

The  deployment  workflow  separates  weak-supervision  label  construction  from  local  inference.  The  LLM generates training labels during model development. After training, a deterministic personal-information module and CPU-deployable student process new records. Hospitals can inspect redaction rules, model versions, and predictions locally without invoking a cloud LLM for each record.

The model-level benchmark recorded 0.2042 ms per record, indicating that the student is lightweight enough for batch computation on a standard CPU. The complete local benchmark reported a median latency of 4.56 ms and a 95th-percentile latency of 9.73 ms, including redaction, TF-IDF transformation, inference, and decoding. The serialized  model and vectorizer required 22.8 MB. These measurements define the practical basis of the CPU-deployment claim. They do not establish an end-to-end speed advantage over every representation family because equivalent full-pipeline timings were not collected for all embedding baselines.

Information-security  staff  can  inspect  the  deterministic  personal-information  module  and  adapt  it  to  local telephone, identification-number, and address formats. Because the module is separate from the student, privacy rules can be updated without retraining the classification heads. The rule module and student can also be tested separately,  while rule  matches and  model predictions can be logged independently to support error diagnosis and version tracing.

In  routine  use,  hospitals  can  sample  high-priority,  low-confidence,  and  rare-category  records  for  human review.  The  reviewed  records  can  then  be  used  to  update  the  weak-supervision  dataset  and  recalibrate  later model versions, creating a traceable revision history.

## 5.3 Scope and Future Work

Because  the  labels  are  LLM-assisted  weak  supervision,  macro-F1  measures  agreement  with  that  label schema. An independent assessment for routine hospital use should include stratified human review of  highpriority, privacy/security, medication, and other rare categories.

The 2,000 public and de-identified records provide a reproducible basis for method comparison. Hospitalowned complaint systems may differ in wording, severity distribution, and institutional terminology. External validation should cover complaint, survey, hotline, and follow-up records and should deliberately sample rare categories.

The  personal-information  benchmark  measures  agreement  with  the  current  dataset  labels  and  configured patterns. The supplementary synthetic test verified detection and removal for seven configured pattern families. Generic  names,  addresses,  and  institution-specific  identifiers  require  local  pattern  extensions  and  periodic checks based on error logs and uncertain records.

The latency results establish CPU feasibility. A strict end-to-end comparison across representation families requires unified timing of tokenization, embedding, feature construction, preprocessing, and batching.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper presented RC-EdgeDistill, an LLM-supervised redaction-consistent learning method for hospital feedback at the edge. Two LLM instructions with different emphases construct four weak-supervision labels. A compact  multi-task  student  learns  from  paired  original  and  redacted  texts,  and  a  symmetric  KL  objective constrains  semantic  predictions  across  the  two  views.  RC-EdgeDistill  achieved  a  mean  macro-F1  of  0.6896, comparable  with  the  strongest  evaluated  CPU  classifier  and  16.64%  above  the  best-performing  frozen embedding baseline. Redaction consistency reached 98.67%, compared with 95.33% for the corresponding CPU baseline. The model and vectorizer occupied 22.8 MB. The complete local pipeline had a median latency of 4.56 ms per  record,  and  95%  of  records  were  processed  within  9.73  ms.  The  findings  indicate  that  LLM-derived supervision  can  train  a  compact  model  for  standard  CPUs  and  that  explicit  consistency  learning  reduces prediction  changes  caused  by  masking  personal  identifiers.  By  separating  label  construction  from  routine inference,  RC-EdgeDistill  provides  a  reproducible,  auditable,  and  maintainable  edge-analysis  method  for hospital feedback. Stratified human review and validation on hospital-owned feedback will further establish its applicability across local channels and institutional terminology.

## Data Availability Statement

The final analysis used 951 unique English reviews retained after duplicate removal from version 1 of the Hospital Reviews Dataset by junaid6731 [43]. The dataset is available from Kaggle at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/junaid6731/hospital-reviews-dataset  under  the  CC  BY-SA  4.0  license.  Its documentation states that the reviews were collected from Google Maps pages for hospitals in Bengaluru, India. The  remaining  1,049  records  were  sampled  from  publicly  accessible  Chinese  web  content  collected  by  the authors using monitoring software. The analytic workflow excluded profile URLs, source URLs, and accountlevel metadata and applied identifier masking before modeling. The English source dataset, the authors' publicweb source archive, and repository documentation are available at https://github.com/intraweb19790700/Hospital.  No  restricted  patient-level  clinical  records  were  used  in  this study.
